Drift or Selection

Four arms, thirty seeds each, twelve thousand ticks. Asking whether plant defense actually responds to selection — and finding that the honest answer, once the spread is measured rather than eyeballed, is that we cannot yet tell.

Retraction

An earlier write-up in this project claimed these runs showed “plant defense under measurable directional selection.” That claim is withdrawn. It rested on comparing the −0.055 decline against the uniform arms’ deltas, which have small spread by construction — no standing variance means no lineage lottery. Compared against its own sampling error, the decline is t = −1.44 with a 95% interval of [−0.122, +0.032]. The interval contains zero.

Everything below is the corrected reading.

The four arms

Every arm starts at mean defense 0.30. The only difference between the two pairs is whether that mean is made of identical patches (uniform: six sites at 0.30) or varying ones (variance: three at 0.60, three at 0.00). Matched means, differing standing variance — the single variable selection needs to act on.

Every interval crosses zero

The change in mean plant defense from first sample to last, per arm, with a 95% bootstrap interval over the thirty seeds. A result only counts as a detected response if its interval clears the dashed line.

The two variance arms sit lowest, and they are the two with any claim to a response — but both intervals reach across zero. The uniform arms are tight and centred on nothing, which is what a true null looks like here.

Why the means were misleading

Each faint line is one seed. The heavy line is the mean across all thirty. This is the chart that makes the retraction obvious: the variance arms’ mean drifts down while individual seeds fan out across almost the entire trait range, some ending near zero and some above 0.55.

variance (3 sites @ 0.60, 3 @ 0.00) uniform (6 sites @ 0.30) starting mean 0.30

With roughly six to thirty patches, the plant population is small enough that lineage fixation by drift moves the mean further than selection plausibly could. Standing variance triples the outcome spread — SD 0.208 against 0.069 — which is drift being given room, not a directional signal.

Deterrence works mechanically, and changes nothing evolutionarily

variance uniform deterrence on

Realized grazing pressure — biomass removed per unit standing biomass per second. The deterrence arms sit measurably lower, so PlantDefenseDeterrenceEnabled is doing exactly what it was built to do. It simply does not convert into a fitness difference large enough to see.

The ecosystem was healthy throughout

variance uniform deterrence on

This matters because an earlier attempt produced a large defense shift only in runs where every seed went extinct. Here population holds at the cap of 48 and plants turn over fifteen generations in all four arms, with zero extinctions across 120 runs. The null is a null from a working ecosystem, not from a collapsed one.

What would actually settle it

The obstacle is statistical power, and it has a specific cause: the plant population is tiny, so drift dominates. Three routes, in order of directness:

  • More patches. Drift scales inversely with population size. A scenario with hundreds of sites instead of six would shrink the seed-to-seed spread far more effectively than more seeds would.
  • More seeds. At SD 0.21 and an effect near −0.055, detecting it at 80% power needs roughly 230 seeds rather than 30 — about eight times the compute, and only worth spending if the effect is believed real.
  • Remove the confound first. Defense costs growth (−.15f × Defense) independently of grazing. Run the same scenario with grazing suppressed: if defense still falls by −0.05, the growth cost explains all of it and grazing contributes nothing, which would make the coevolution question moot at these settings.

Until one of those runs, the correct statement is that this setup cannot yet distinguish selection on plant defense from drift — which also means the P4 blocker it was meant to clear is still open.
